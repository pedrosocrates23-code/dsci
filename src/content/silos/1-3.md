## What a CPSR is

A Cosmetic Product Safety Report is the written evidence that a cosmetic product is safe for human health. Article 3 of Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 sets the standard: a product on the EU market must be safe when used under normal or reasonably foreseeable conditions. Article 10 says how that safety is shown. Before the product is placed on the market, the responsible person must make sure it has undergone a safety assessment and that a safety report is set up following Annex I.

Annex I lists what the report must contain "as a minimum," in two parts:

- **Part A, cosmetic product safety information.** The data: composition, stability, microbiology, impurities, exposure, toxicology and known adverse effects.
- **Part B, cosmetic product safety assessment.** The judgment: a qualified safety assessor reads Part A and states whether the product is safe.

The European Commission published guidelines on Annex I as Implementing Decision 2013/674/EU. They call the report "an expert piece of work made up of different modules." They also ask that it use the same or similar headings as Annex I, so competent authorities can find each item quickly.

The CPSR is one of the documents behind our [toxicological risk assessment services](/services/toxicological-risk-assessment/). For cosmetics headed to the EU, it has to exist before the product is placed on the market.

## Who needs a CPSR

### The responsible person

Every cosmetic product on the EU market needs a responsible person, a legal or natural person designated within the EU. Article 4 decides who that is:

- For a product made in the EU, the manufacturer established there, unless it designates someone else by written mandate.
- For a product made in the EU by a manufacturer established outside it, a person in the EU that the manufacturer designates by written mandate.
- For an imported product, each importer, for the product it places on the market.
- A distributor, when it sells the product under its own name or trademark, or changes it in a way that could affect compliance.

The responsible person owns the CPSR obligation, and Article 10 adds three duties. The assessment must consider the intended use and the expected systemic exposure to each ingredient. It must use an appropriate weight-of-evidence approach for data from all existing sources. And it must stay up to date after launch.

### Great Britain and Northern Ireland

In Great Britain, the rules come from Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 as amended by the Product Safety and Metrology etc. (Amendment etc.) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019. UK government guidance on them says the responsible person must have a UK established address and must keep a product information file in English. That file includes the cosmetic product safety report, again in two parts: Part A provided by the responsible person and Part B by the safety assessor. Northern Ireland is outside that guidance and has separate technical guidance of its own.

## How the CPSR, the PIF and the CPNP fit together

These three acronyms show up together, and they're easy to mix up.

**The product information file (PIF)** is the full dossier for one product, defined in Article 11. The CPSR sits inside it, next to four other items: a description that ties the file to the product, the manufacturing method with a statement on good manufacturing practice, proof of the claimed effect where the product's nature or effect justifies it, and data on any animal testing. The responsible person keeps the PIF for ten years after the last batch is placed on the market. It must be readily accessible to the competent authority at the address on the label, in a language that authority can easily understand.

**The Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (CPNP)** is the European Commission's free online system for the notification Article 13 requires. Before the product is placed on the market, the responsible person submits a short list of facts. It covers the product's category and name, the address where the PIF is kept and the country of origin for imports. It also covers the Member State of sale, a contact person, any nanomaterials, any substances classified as CMR category 1A or 1B, and the frame formulation. Once a product is notified in the CPNP, no further notification at national level is needed within the EU.

The safety report itself isn't on that notification list. It stays in the PIF, where the authority can inspect it.

## What goes into Part A

Annex I gives Part A ten headings. In short:

1. Composition: the qualitative and quantitative formula, with the chemical identity of each substance (chemical name, INCI, CAS, EINECS/ELINCS where possible) and its function. For a perfume, the composition's name, code number and supplier.
2. Physical and chemical characteristics, and stability: data for the substances, the mixtures and the product, and the product's stability under reasonably foreseeable storage conditions.
3. Microbiological quality: specifications for raw materials and product, plus the results of the preservation challenge test. Products used around the eyes, on mucous membranes or damaged skin, on children under three, on elderly people or on people with compromised immune responses get particular attention.
4. Impurities, traces and packaging: the purity of substances and mixtures, evidence that any trace of a prohibited substance is technically unavoidable, and the purity and stability of the packaging material.
5. Normal and reasonably foreseeable use: how the product will be used, read in light of the label warnings.
6. Exposure to the product: site and surface area of application, amount applied, duration and frequency of use, exposure routes and the exposed population. Secondary exposure counts too, such as inhaling a spray or swallowing a lip product.
7. Exposure to the substances: the exposure to each substance for the relevant toxicological endpoints.
8. Toxicological profile of the substances: all relevant endpoints, with a focus on skin and eye irritation, skin sensitization and, for UV-absorbing substances, photo-induced toxicity. Any read-across must be justified, and every source identified.
9. Undesirable effects and serious undesirable effects: all available data for the product and, where relevant, for other cosmetic products.
10. Other information: for example, existing studies in human volunteers.

Part A is a minimum, and the Commission guidelines explain how to handle gaps. If an item isn't relevant to a given product, the absence must be justified in Part A, and the safety assessor must repeat and validate that justification in Part B. The guidelines give the preservation challenge test as an example: products with low microbiological risk, such as those with more than 20% alcohol, don't need one, though a scientific justification is still required.

### The margin of safety

For systemic effects, Annex I asks for a margin of safety (MoS) based on a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL). Any decision not to calculate it must be justified. The SCCS Notes of Guidance give the formula for threshold effects: the systemic point of departure divided by the systemic exposure dose. When no NOAEL is available, the Commission guidelines allow other reference values, such as a NOEL or a LOAEL, to calculate the margin of safety. A benchmark dose may also be used, provided it's compared with the exposure from the cosmetic.

## What goes into Part B

Part B is where the safety assessor reaches a conclusion. Annex I gives it four sections:

1. **Assessment conclusion.** A statement on the product's safety under Article 3. The Commission guidelines say it should state whether the product is safe, safe with restrictions, or not safe.
2. **Labelled warnings and instructions of use.** Which particular warnings and instructions the label needs.
3. **Reasoning.** The scientific explanation that leads from Part A to the conclusion. Margins of safety are discussed where relevant. Products for children under three and products used only for external intimate hygiene get a specific assessment. Possible interactions between substances, and the effect of stability on safety, must be considered.
4. **Assessor's credentials and approval.** The safety assessor's name and address, proof of qualification, and the date and signature.

The guidelines call the reasoning "the core of the safety assessment." The assessor can accept the formulation, reject it, or accept it under specific conditions.

## The safety assessor and Article 10(2)

Article 10(2) sets the legal qualification for Part B. The person who carries it out must hold a diploma or other evidence of formal qualifications. That diploma comes from a university course of theoretical and practical study in pharmacy, toxicology, medicine or a similar discipline, or from a course a Member State recognizes as equivalent.

The Commission guidelines are firm on this point. They say the qualified safety assessor "should remain the only professional allowed to carry out the cosmetic product safety assessment" in Part B. They also expect the responsible person and the assessor to work closely. A responsible person who isn't the manufacturer may need input from the manufacturer, the raw material suppliers and other technical experts.

Two more rules shape the data an assessor can use:

- Good laboratory practice: Article 10(3) covers nonclinical safety studies run after 30 June 1988 for a cosmetic safety assessment. They must follow the EU principles of good laboratory practice, or an international standard that the Commission or ECHA recognizes as equivalent.
- Animal testing: The guidelines ask the assessor to make sure experimental data comply with Article 18 of the Regulation, which governs animal testing.

## When the report must be updated

Article 10 doesn't set a validity period for the CPSR. It requires the report to be kept up to date as new relevant information appears after launch. The Commission guidelines say the product's safety should be reassessed regularly and reviewed when:

- new scientific findings or toxicological data could change the result;
- the formulation or the raw material specifications change;
- the conditions of use change;
- undesirable effects show a rising trend in nature, severity or frequency.

A change in the law, such as a new restriction on an ingredient, also calls for a check of the formulation and the labelling.

## The guidance safety assessors work from

Two documents guide the work alongside the Regulation. One is Implementing Decision 2013/674/EU, the Commission's guidelines on Annex I quoted throughout this page. The other is the SCCS Notes of Guidance for the Testing of Cosmetic Ingredients and their Safety Evaluation. The SCCS says they apply in principle to safety assessors in industry as much as to the committee itself. The version the SCCS page lists today is the 12th revision (SCCS/1647/22), adopted on 15 May 2023, with corrigenda on 26 October and 21 December 2023. The Notes were written for the substances in the Regulation's annexes, but the SCCS says they can also be of practical use when preparing a PIF for a finished product.
